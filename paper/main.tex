\title{The Privacy Externality Game:\\
Strategic Budget Choices in Sampling-Based Individual Differential Privacy}

\author{
\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
}

\begin{abstract}
Individual differential privacy (iDP) promises each data contributor
control over their own privacy: a personal budget $(\eps_i, \delta)$,
chosen by them, enforced for them. The dominant realisation of that
promise in deep learning---sampling-based iDP---keeps a single noise
multiplier for the whole dataset and turns each budget into a
per-record sampling rate, so every contributor's realised
membership-inference risk depends on the budget choices of everyone
else. If risk is collectively determined, budget choice is a game, and
the right question is not whether the mechanism \emph{can} be abused
but what \emph{rational} contributors will do with it. We formalise
this game as the Privacy Externality Game (PEG), prove that the
externality is monotone---tightening any budget raises everyone else's
realised risk---and characterise its equilibria. Our headline finding
is that the game generically refuses symmetric play: wherever the
shared model utility is not yet saturated, every symmetric profile
is a local \emph{minimum} of each player's own utility, and stable
play is asymmetric---in the canonical instance, $42$ of $43$ pure
equilibria put one player roughly $6.5\times$ looser, with a
strictly higher realised risk compensated by the shared gain in
model utility. Symmetric play survives only on a knife-edge: a
single symmetric pure equilibrium at the budget where utility
saturation sets in, which is also the equilibrium with the least
aggregate risk. Heterogeneous privacy preferences do not soften the
asymmetry; they sort players into protected and exposed roles, with
the exposed role going to whoever values privacy least. The asymmetry is a
property of the sampling-based realisation, not of iDP itself: the
sensitivity-based realisation, replayed through the same game, has a
unique and symmetric equilibrium. A recent budget-manipulation attack
on iDP emerges in our framework as the coordinated limit of ordinary
rational play---and collapses by a factor of 18--53 when the target is
allowed to best-respond. Throughout, risk is priced
mechanism-specifically: with the strongest adversary the deployed
mechanism admits, not the loose mechanism-agnostic
$(\eps,\delta)$ bound; a measured shadow-model instance locates the
boundary of the phenomenon, which vanishes where realised attack
power sits at the measurement floor.
Because no report-based fix exists (we show
the mechanism is not strategyproof), mitigation must change the
contract itself; a $(\eps_i,\delta,\Delta)$-iDP contract provably caps
the externality any coalition can inflict.
\end{abstract}
