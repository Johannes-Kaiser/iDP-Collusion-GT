\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The right to privacy is personal, and privacy preferences are
heterogeneous: a clinical patient and a casual app user contributing
records to the same model place very different values on not being
identifiable from it. Classical \ac{DP}~\cite{dwork2006calibrating} ignores this heterogeneity---a single
budget $(\eps,\delta)$ covers every record. \ac{iDP}~\cite{jorgensen2015conservative,
boenisch2023have, feldman2021individual} restores it: each record
receives its own contract $(\eps_i, \delta)$, chosen to match its
owner's preference. The promise is individual control. This paper is
about how that promise fares once the contributors holding those
budgets are allowed to choose them rationally.

The promise meets reality in the mechanism that delivers it. The
dominant deep-learning realisation of \ac{iDP}, the sampling-based
mechanism of \citet{boenisch2023have}, keeps a \emph{single} noise
multiplier $\sigma$ for the whole training run and translates each
record's budget into a personal Poisson sampling rate $p_i$,
calibrated so that every contract holds at equality under a fixed
expected batch size. The shared $\sigma$ and the fixed batch size
couple every sampling rate to every budget: when one contributor
tightens their budget, the mechanism must re-solve, and everyone else's
sampling rate---and with it their \emph{realised} \ac{MIA} risk---moves. \citet{kaiser2026your} demonstrated this
empirically and weaponised it: an adversary controlling other records'
budgets can inflate a target's \ac{MIA} susceptibility while every formal
$(\eps_i,\delta)$ guarantee continues to hold. Individual privacy
risk, under this realisation, is collectively determined.

\paragraph{This is a game.} A choice whose consequences are determined
by everyone's choices is, by definition, the subject of game theory.
Prior work treats the collective determination of \ac{iDP} risk as an
attack surface: something a malicious coalition exploits against a
victim~\cite{kaiser2026your}. We ask the question that comes before
any attack: \emph{what do ordinary, self-interested contributors do
when they understand the coupling?} Each player values the shared
model and dislikes their own realised \ac{MIA} risk---priced, throughout,
mechanism-specifically: the most any adversary can extract from the
mechanism actually deployed, tighter than the mechanism-agnostic
$(\eps,\delta)$ worst case and above any concrete
attack (\cref{sec:mia}); each knows that
tightening their budget exports risk onto the others while degrading the
model for all. We call the externality at the heart of this trade-off
the \emph{privacy externality} and the resulting normal-form game the
\ac{PEG}. Its equilibria---not any
adversary---are our object of study.

The answer is unsettling. The externality must land on someone: the
mechanism's batch constraint conserves aggregate sampling exposure,
so the game never decides \emph{whether} the exposure is held, only
\emph{by whom}. At every pure Nash equilibrium of the symmetric
two-player game, one player carries a strictly higher realised \ac{MIA}
advantage than the other, and no symmetric profile is a pure
equilibrium at all: at any symmetric candidate, each player's utility
has a local \emph{minimum} in their own action, so conforming is the
one thing rational players will not do. The game behaves like the
classical Hawk--Dove game~\cite{maynardsmith1973logic}: the roles are
strategic substitutes, deviations from symmetry amplify rather than
damp back, the only symmetric equilibrium is a mixed-strategy lottery
over who is exposed, and who ends up exposed at a pure equilibrium is
decided by equilibrium selection, not by anyone's contract. When preferences are
heterogeneous the roles stop being arbitrary---the equilibrium
\emph{sorts} players, assigning the exposed role to whoever values
privacy least. Risk under sampling-based \ac{iDP} is not merely
interdependent, as prior work observed; at equilibrium it is
systematically \emph{redistributed}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The main contributions of our work are as
follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We formalise sampling-based \ac{iDP} budget choice as the $n$-player
  Privacy Externality Game and prove the externality is monotone:
  tightening any budget raises every other player's realised \ac{MIA}
  advantage, shrinking their slack below the \ac{DP} ceiling.
\item We prove the symmetric two-player game has no interior
  symmetric pure equilibrium---the diagonal is a trough of own
  utility, and budgets are strategic substitutes, so deviations
  amplify; symmetry survives only as a mixed lottery over roles, and
  heterogeneous preferences sort the exposed role onto whoever values
  privacy least.
\item We show the asymmetry is mechanism-specific---the
  sensitivity-based realisation yields a unique, symmetric
  equilibrium---and recover the budget-manipulation attack of
  \citet{kaiser2026your} as rational coalition play, which collapses
  $18$--$35\times$ against a target that best-responds.
\item We quantify the welfare cost of equilibrium play (the price of
  externality-vulnerability, $\PoEV$),
  prove that budget declaration is not strategyproof (no reporting
  discipline can repair the mechanism from inside), and prove that a
  $(\eps_i, \delta, \Delta)$-iDP contract caps at $\Delta$ the risk
  movement any coalition can inflict.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implication.} The attack literature on iDP frames excess
vulnerability as something an adversary inflicts. Our equilibrium
analysis says the adversary is optional: asymmetric exposure is what
rational play produces on its own, and the coalition attack is merely
its coordinated limit. Mitigation therefore cannot proceed by
detecting attackers---there may be none---but must change the contract
so that the game itself stops admitting harmful equilibria.
