\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Differential privacy}
\label{sec:dp}

A randomised mechanism $\mathcal{M}$ is $(\eps,
\delta)$-differentially private
($(\eps,\delta)$-DP)~\cite{dwork2006calibrating} if, for all adjacent
datasets $D \sim D'$ and all measurable output sets $S$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^\eps \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] +
\delta .
\label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
\ac{DPSGD} instantiates \cref{eq:dp} for deep learning: per-example
gradients are clipped, their sum is perturbed with Gaussian noise
$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, and Poisson subsampling---each record
enters each batch independently with probability $p$---amplifies the
per-step guarantee before composition over training
iterations~\cite{abadi2016deep}. A record's protection is therefore
set by two dials: the noise multiplier $\sigma$, shared by all
records, and its own sampling rate $p$.

\subsection{Individual differential privacy}
\label{sec:idp}

A single $(\eps, \delta)$ imposes one privacy level on contributors
whose dispositions demonstrably differ: three decades of surveys
following Westin consistently segment any surveyed population into
privacy fundamentalists, pragmatists, and
unconcerned~\cite{kumaraguru2005privacy}, and the split persists
among users of emerging health
technologies~\cite{schairer2019disposition}, even though the
categories predict individual behaviour only
loosely~\cite{woodruff2014would}. We write $\lambda_i > 0$ for the
utility/privacy weight contributor $i$ attributes to their data: how
much they value a unit of their own realised leakage relative to a
unit of model quality. The survey evidence says $\lambda_i$ varies
widely within any one dataset---a patient with a stigmatised
diagnosis and a healthy volunteer in the same clinical study, or a
rare-disease clinic and a large research hospital in the same
cross-silo federation~\cite{kairouz2021advances,
mcmahan2017communication, liu2024crosssilo}.
\ac{iDP}~\cite{jorgensen2015conservative} operationalises this
heterogeneity, giving record $i$ its own budget: $\mathcal{M}$ is
$(\eps_i, \delta)$-iDP if for every $i$,
every pair $D \sim_i D'$ differing only in record $i$, and all $S$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D) \in S] \;\leq\; e^{\eps_i} \Pr[\mathcal{M}(D') \in S] +
\delta .
\label{eq:idp}
\end{equation}
Realisations fall into two families~\cite{feldman2021individual,
boenisch2023have}. \emph{Accounting} realisations track each record's
cumulative expenditure and filter it out once its budget is
exhausted; the progressive record removal causes catastrophic
forgetting and poor utility~\cite{feldman2021individual,
boenisch2023have}. \emph{Assignment} realisations instead fix
per-record training parameters up front: \emph{sensitivity-based} \ac{iDP}
assigns each record a personal clipping norm, \emph{sampling-based}
\ac{iDP} a personal Poisson sampling rate under one shared noise
multiplier. Sampling-based \ac{iDP} attains the better empirical utility
of the two~\cite{boenisch2023have, kaiser2026your}; we adopt the
formulation of \citet{boenisch2023have} and use the sensitivity-based
variant as a control (\cref{sec:scale}).

\begin{definition}[Sampling-based iDP-SGD~\cite{boenisch2023have}]
\label[definition]{def:sampling-idp}
Let the dataset of size $M$ partition into $G$ \emph{privacy groups}
$\{G_1,\dots,G_G\}$ with sizes $|G_g|$. Each group has a target
$(\eps_g,\delta)$-DP budget. The mechanism trains by Poisson-subsampled
\ac{DPSGD} for $I$ iterations, where the noise multiplier $\sigma$ and the
per-group sampling rates $p_1,\dots,p_G$ are chosen such that
\begin{align}
\textnormal{(Budget)} \quad
& \textnormal{for every } g\textnormal{, the } I\textnormal{-fold } (p_g,\sigma)\textnormal{-sub-} \notag\\
& \textnormal{sampled Gaussian is } (\eps_g,\delta)\textnormal{-DP at equality;} \notag\\
\textnormal{(Batch)} \quad
& \sum_{g=1}^G \frac{|G_g|}{M}\,p_g \;=\; \frac{B}{M}, \label{eq:batch}
\end{align}
where $B$ is the desired expected batch size. We refer to $(\sigma,
p_1,\dots,p_G)$ as the \emph{mechanism solution} for the budget vector
$\boldsymbol{\eps} = (\eps_1,\dots,\eps_G)$.
\end{definition}

We account for the subsampled-Gaussian composition in \ac{RDP}~\cite{mironov2017renyi, abadi2016deep} for tight composition, but
state each contract in $(\eps_i, \delta)$ form, following
\citet{boenisch2023have, kaiser2026your}. The budget $(\eps, \delta)$
is the formal quantification of a record's information
leakage~\cite{dwork2006calibrating}; it is the basis of the contract
between a data contributor and the party that processes their
data~\cite{kaiser2026your}, and the form in which guarantees are
deployed and communicated in practice~\cite{cummings2024advancing}.

\subsection{Membership inference, privacy profiles, and slack}
\label{sec:mia}

A contract $(\eps, \delta)$ is a lossy description of a mechanism's
leakage. In \cref{eq:dp} the two parameters compensate for each
other---a larger $\eps$ lets the same mechanism claim a smaller
$\delta$---so a fixed mechanism satisfies \cref{eq:dp} not at one
pair but along a whole curve: its \emph{privacy profile} $\{(\eps,
\delta(\eps)) : \eps \geq 0\}$, where $\delta(\eps)$ is the smallest
$\delta$ at which \cref{eq:dp} holds~\cite{balle2020privacy}. The
profile describes the leakage completely; the contract is a single
point on it, and mechanisms through the same point can have very
different profile shapes~\cite{kaissis2024beyond, gomez2025harmful,
kaiser2026your}. The risk a contributor actually faces is determined
by that shape, not by the calibration point.

To make risk operational we use \ac{MIA}: an
adversary observing the mechanism's output tests whether a target
record was in the training set~\cite{yeom2018privacy,
carlini2022membership}. \ac{DP} is itself a hypothesis-testing guarantee,
and the profile is equivalent to a \emph{trade-off curve}
$T(\alpha)$: the least type-II error at type-I level $\alpha$ that
any test---hence any \ac{MIA} adversary---can
achieve~\cite{dong2022gaussian}. The adversary's best operating
point defines the advantage,
\begin{equation}
\Adv(\mathcal{M}) \;=\; \sup_{\alpha \in [0,1]}
\bigl(\,1 - \alpha - T(\alpha)\,\bigr),
\label{eq:adv}
\end{equation}
the largest gap between true-positive and false-positive rate. We
compute the trade-off curve of the $I$-fold $(p,\sigma)$-subsampled
Gaussian exactly by numerical
accounting~\cite{google-dp-accounting} and read the advantage off
\cref{eq:adv}.

\paragraph{The advantage hierarchy.} The paper moves between three
notions of vulnerability, one per level of knowledge about the
mechanism.
\begin{itemize}
\item $A_{\eps,\delta}$ \emph{(contract level, mechanism-agnostic).}
  Knowing only the contract, the worst case over every mechanism it
  admits is $A_{\eps,\delta} :=
  (e^{\eps}-1+2\delta)/(e^{\eps}+1)$~\cite{kairouz2015composition,
  kaissis2024beyond}: no $(\eps,\delta)$-DP mechanism, whatever its
  form, can leak more. As a worst case over profiles of every shape,
  it is generally loose for any particular mechanism.
\item $A_{\mathrm{PLD}}$ \emph{(mechanism level).} Knowing the
  deployed mechanism---the actual sampling rates, Gaussian noise, and
  composition, resolved by the exact \ac{PLD}
  accountant, whence the name---its trade-off curve gives the
  advantage \cref{eq:adv} of the \emph{optimal} adversary: a true
  upper bound on what any attacker, with any decision rule, can
  extract from that mechanism. This is the vulnerability our game
  prices; player $i$'s vulnerability $\Apld{i}$ is always this
  quantity.
\item $A_{\mathrm{LiRA}}$ \emph{(attack level).} The realised success
  of a concrete attack against one trained model. The
  \ac{LiRA}~\cite{carlini2022membership}
  instantiates the membership hypothesis test with shadow models and
  is currently among the most powerful attacks that are realistic
  and applicable---the practical means by which an adversary
  leverages the mechanism's information leakage. Realised per-record
  vulnerability at a fixed budget is highly
  heterogeneous~\cite{carlini2022privacy}.
\end{itemize}
The three levels are ordered:
\begin{equation}
A_{\eps,\delta} \;\geq\; A_{\mathrm{PLD}} \;\gtrsim\; A_{\mathrm{LiRA}}.
\label{eq:hierarchy}
\end{equation}
The first inequality holds by construction---level 2 evaluates one
mechanism inside the level-1 worst case. Its gap,
\[
\mathrm{slack} \;:=\; A_{\eps, \delta} - A_{\mathrm{PLD}}
\;\geq\; 0 ,
\]
is the headroom between what the contract permits and what the
mechanism actually leaks; the $\Delta$-divergence of
\citet{kaissis2024beyond} makes this gap decision-theoretic,
quantifying as an approximate Blackwell dominance the worst-case
excess vulnerability of running one mechanism in place of another at
the same contract. The second inequality is not a theorem: an attack
that is not information-theoretically optimal should achieve no more
than the optimal adversary~\cite{kaissis2023optimal}, but the
trade-off curve is computed for a model of the implemented
mechanism---training dynamics, finite precision, and implementation
detail are abstracted away---so we treat $A_{\mathrm{PLD}} \gtrsim
A_{\mathrm{LiRA}}$ as an empirical regularity.
\Cref{fig:hierarchy} makes the first two levels

\paragraph{Where the externality appears.} In sampling-based iDP the
budget vector selects the mechanism, and \cref{fig:hierarchy} shows
how much that selection matters. The shared $\sigma$ in (Budget) is
the channel: for any candidate $\sigma$, the largest sampling rate
that meets group $g$'s budget increases in $\sigma$ (more noise,
more room to sample), and (Batch) then pins $\sigma$ as the unique
value at which the rates sum to $B$. When any group tightens its
budget the mechanism re-solves, and every other group's $(p_g,
\sigma)$---its trade-off curve, its advantage, its slack---moves
along exactly the family of mechanisms in \cref{fig:hierarchy} while
every contract point stays put. Record $i$'s realised risk is thus a
function of \emph{everyone's} choices, while its contract mentions
only $\eps_i$. Fixing the expected batch size is only one way to
aggregate the per-record budgets into a shared $\sigma$; the
externality is not specific to it but is inherent to any informed
choice of a shared noise multiplier~\cite{kaiser2026your}, so the
game we build applies to any such rule. \citet{kaiser2026your}
demonstrated the stakes: adversarial budget choices inflate the \ac{MIA}
susceptibility of $62\%$ of targeted records without touching any
contract, and a $(\eps_i, \delta, \Delta)$-iDP clause capping the
slack was proposed as the remedy. We take the complementary route:
rather than restricting the mechanism, we explain the realised excess
vulnerability as the equilibrium of self-interested play under the
current contract---and return to the $\Delta$-clause as mitigation in
\cref{sec:mitigation}.

\subsection{Strategic privacy: games, externalities, incentives}
\label{sec:related}

A normal-form game $\langle N, \Sigma, U\rangle$ consists of players
$N = \{1,\dots,n\}$, action sets $\Sigma = \{S_1, \dots, S_n\}$, and
payoffs $U = \{u_1,\dots,u_n\}$. A \ac{NE} is a
profile at which no player gains from unilateral
deviation~\cite{nash1950equilibrium}; a \ac{BR}
maximises a player's utility against fixed opponent play; the
\emph{price of anarchy} compares worst-equilibrium welfare to the
social optimum~\cite{koutsoupias1999worst}; in a Stackelberg game a
leader commits before followers best-respond. Two classical templates
organise our results. \emph{Hawk--Dove}~\cite{maynardsmith1973logic}
is the canonical symmetric game whose pure equilibria are all
asymmetric: someone must yield, and the equilibrium---not anyone's
preference---decides who. Shared-resource
games~\cite{hardin1968tragedy} show how a common constraint (here,
the shared $\sigma$ under a fixed batch size) turns each player's
private choice into an externality~\cite{buchanan1962externality} on
all others.

\paragraph{Privacy games.} \citet{pejo2019together} introduced the
closest predecessor to our game: a two-player collaborative-learning
game in which each player chooses a privacy parameter and the
\emph{Price of Privacy} measures the accuracy lost at equilibrium.
In their mechanism, however, a player's privacy choice affects only
their own \emph{privacy} and both players' \emph{accuracy}: the privacy
cost is separable, which makes their game a potential
game~\cite{monderer1996potential} and its analysis a one-dimensional
minimisation. In sampling-based \ac{iDP} every player's privacy choice
moves every other player's \emph{privacy}---a qualitatively stronger
coupling that destroys the potential structure
(\cref{prop:non-potential}). Earlier game-theoretic privacy work
studies different couplings: \citet{ioannidis2013linear} model
regression with private features as a non-cooperative game,
\citet{chessa2015game} study non-monetary incentives in data
analytics, and \citet{pawlick2016stackelberg} model the
learner-vs-perturber conflict as a Stackelberg game. None exhibit the
privacy-to-privacy externality of a shared sampling mechanism. On the
mechanism-design side, selling privacy as a good with heterogeneous
valuations traces to \citet{ghosh2011selling}, with truthfulness
impossibilities for unbounded privacy
valuations~\cite{nissim2014redrawing, chen2013truthful}; we show in
\cref{app:mechanism-design} why these positive results do not rescue
budget declaration in the \ac{PEG}.

\paragraph{Where the game lives.} The \ac{PEG} lives in systems that
individualise privacy budgets and solve for a shared noise multiplier
from the resulting budget vector. The budgets can be
record-specific---a training instance running sampling-based
\ac{iDP} over its own dataset~\cite{boenisch2023have}---or
silo-specific, as in cross-silo federated learning with personalised
budgets~\cite{liu2024crosssilo, mcmahan2017communication,
geyer2017differentially, kairouz2021advances}, where the
silos---hospitals in a consortium, banks in a fraud-detection
coalition---are exactly the separately identifiable entities holding
heterogeneous preferences. A separate literature designs
incentives on top of privacy-heterogeneous federated
learning~\cite{li2024fedpcs, donahue2021optimality, tu2025idhfl,
ma2024evolutionary}; our equilibrium characterisation is the baseline
any such scheme must improve upon---it is what happens when no
incentive is offered.
