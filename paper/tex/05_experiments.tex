\section{Numerical Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

The theory of \cref{sec:analysis} makes qualitative predictions:
asymmetric equilibria, ordered by privacy weight, specific to the
sampling-based mechanism. We now quantify them. The evaluation asks
five questions, each a subsection: whether the symmetric game has a
symmetric equilibrium (\cref{sec:C1}); who absorbs the externality
under size and preference asymmetry (\cref{sec:C2}); whether the
picture survives more players (\cref{sec:H3}); whether it is the
mechanism or the paradigm (\cref{sec:scale}); and what equilibrium
play costs, both per contract (\cref{sec:C5}) and in aggregate
(\cref{sec:poev}). \Cref{sec:H1} closes by asking whether any of it
survives a \emph{measured}, rather than analytical, adversary---the
answer draws the practical boundary of the phenomenon. Supporting
sweeps live in the appendices: common-weight shifts
(\cref{app:wsweep}), the raw externality response and its
finite-difference sign certificate (\cref{app:certificate}),
model-utility ablations (\cref{app:v-shape}), and grid and threshold
sensitivity (\cref{app:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Common setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All experiments in
\cref{sec:C1,sec:C2,sec:H3,sec:scale,sec:C5,sec:poev} are
analytical---no model is trained---and share one pipeline. We solve
the mechanism of \cref{def:sampling-idp} by bisecting on $\sigma$
until the batch constraint (Batch) is met, with each per-group rate
chosen to make (Budget) bind under the R\'enyi
accountant~\cite{mironov2017renyi}; profiles where any $p_i$ falls
below a participation threshold are flagged infeasible
(\cref{ass:participation}, \cref{app:mechanism}). Each realised $\Apld{i}$
is then computed exactly from the privacy profile of the $I$-fold
$(p_i,\sigma)$-subsampled Gaussian by \ac{PLD}
accounting~\cite{google-dp-accounting}---the level-2 advantage of
\eqref{eq:hierarchy}, not the level-1 ceiling. The canonical action grid is
log-spaced with $K = 200$ points on $[0.5, 64]$ at $\delta =
10^{-12}$; unless noted otherwise, groups are symmetric with $|G_g| =
5000$, $B = 128$, $5$ training epochs, $\lambda_i = 1$, and $V(\sigma) =
1/(1+\sigma^2)$, mirroring the empirical \ac{iDP} scenarios of
\citet{kaiser2026your, boenisch2023have}. \Cref{sec:H1} instead
trains real \ac{DPSGD} shadow models and measures the advantage with
\ac{LiRA}~\cite{carlini2022membership}.

\subsection{Does the symmetric game have a symmetric equilibrium?}
\label{sec:C1}

We start with the cleanest possible setting: two players, identical
group sizes, identical weights, both budgets swept over the full grid,
pure \acp{NE} enumerated by brute force.

No. The $K = 200$ payoff matrix has $48$ pure Nash equilibria, and
every one of them sits off the diagonal (\cref{fig:C6};
representative equilibria in \cref{tab:NE}). The equilibrium budget
ratio $\max(\eps_1^*,\eps_2^*)/\min(\eps_1^*,\eps_2^*)$ ranges from
$1.02\times$ to $6.54\times$ with median $2.03\times$: even the
equilibria closest to symmetry keep the players on different budgets,
and the realised advantages split accordingly---at the most asymmetric
equilibria one player carries $A$ up to $0.32$ while the other sits
below $0.001$. Because the players are interchangeable, the
equilibria come in mirror pairs, exactly the Hawk--Dove signature
anticipated by \cref{prop:no-symmetric-ne}: both players prefer the
protected role, both cannot have it, and every stable resolution
assigns the roles asymmetrically.

\Cref{fig:C1} dissects why. At any diagonal profile, lowering one's
own budget buys a large drop in own advantage at a modest shared
utility cost---the deviation the externality subsidises, since part of
the bill lands on the opponent. Both players face this incentive
simultaneously, so the diagonal cannot hold; the best-response curves
(\cref{fig:C6}, left) consequently avoid the diagonal and cross only
off it, along a band where one player has committed to the low-budget
role. The mechanics---the diagonal as a payoff trough, the
substitutes structure that keeps deviations from damping back, and
the mixed lottery that is all the symmetry the game retains---are
dissected in \cref{sec:no-symmetric-ne} and quantified in
\cref{app:symbreak}.

Grid resolution moves the equilibrium \emph{count}, not the
structure. At $K = 22$ the same game resolves $8$ pure \acp{NE}, at $K =
200$ it resolves $48$: the stepped \ac{BR} loci of a finer grid cross more
often. What is invariant---and is the claim we stake---is that the \ac{NE}
set stays entirely off-diagonal, in mirror pairs, inside the same
budget-ratio band along the same \ac{BR} loci. We therefore quote $K = 200$
throughout and treat individual \ac{NE} coordinates as samples from the \ac{BR}
crossing band rather than as isolated predictions. The picture is
equally insensitive to how much \emph{everyone} cares: sweeping the
common weight $w$ from $0.125$ to $4$ leaves every equilibrium
off-diagonal ($240$ of $241$; the lone exception sits on a
solver-tolerance artefact) while shifting the cloud toward lower
budgets as privacy weight grows (\cref{app:wsweep}).

\subsection{Who absorbs the externality?}
\label{sec:C2}

Symmetry leaves the exposed role to equilibrium selection. Any
asymmetry assigns it. We break symmetry two ways: by group size and by
preference.

\paragraph{Group size.} Sweeping the size ratio $r = |G_1|/|G_2|$
across two orders of magnitude (all else symmetric), the pattern is
uniform: \emph{the smaller group plays the smaller equilibrium budget
and absorbs the smaller realised advantage} (\cref{fig:C2}). With
player $1$ in the minority ($r<1$), equilibria have $\eps_1^* \in
[1.5, 3]$ against $\eps_2^* \approx 10$, yielding $\Apld{1}^* < 0.01$
against $\Apld{2}^* \in [0.24, 0.32]$; the pattern mirrors for $r > 1$.
The mechanism is legible in (Batch): a small group demanding strong
privacy contributes little to the expected batch, the majority's
sampling rate rises to compensate, and the majority absorbs the
advantage. The minority free-rides on the shared
mechanism~\cite{hardin1968tragedy}---a privacy analogue of the
classical result that small groups benefit disproportionately from a
common resource.

\paragraph{Preference.} Fixing $\lambda_2 = 1$ and sweeping $\lambda_1 \in
\{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$ at $K = 200$ yields $30$--$48$ pure \acp{NE} per
ratio, every one off-diagonal (\cref{fig:C8}). The \ac{NE} cloud migrates
exactly as \cref{prop:self-sorting} predicts: when player $1$ values
privacy less than player $2$, the equilibria sit in the
lower-right region (player $1$ on the high budget, absorbing the
advantage); as $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ crosses unity the cloud migrates through the
symmetric configuration into the upper-left. Heterogeneous preferences
do not meet in a diagonal compromise; the population \emph{splits},
each player settling into the role their weight selects. Sorting
compliance---the fraction of equilibria with the higher-$w$ player on
the lower budget---is $100\%$ across the sweep.

\subsection{Does the asymmetry survive more than two players?}
\label{sec:H3}

We sweep $n \in \{3, \dots, 10\}$ on a coarse $K = 7$ grid,
enumerating pure \acp{NE} exhaustively for $n \le 5$ and running $32$
seeds of Monte-Carlo fictitious play for $n \ge 6$
(\cref{fig:h3-fp-nplayer}). Under heterogeneous weights $\lambda_i \in
\mathrm{geomspace}(0.25, 4)$, the self-sorting signature is exact at
every $n \in \{3,\dots,8\}$---the rank order of equilibrium budgets is
the reverse of the rank order of weights in $100\%$ of
observations---and $83\%$ at $n = 10$, where the fictitious-play tail
occasionally swaps adjacent, nearly-tied weights. Realised advantages
remain strongly asymmetric throughout: the extreme players' advantage
ratio $A_{\max}/A_{\min}$ runs from roughly $480$ at $n = 3$ to $75$
at $n = 10$. Under fully symmetric weights, fictitious play on this coarse
grid settles onto symmetric profiles for $n \le 8$; this is the
expected discretisation effect---the diagonal deviation gain of
\cref{prop:no-symmetric-ne} is second-order and falls below a $K = 7$
grid step---and at $n = 10$ the dynamics again find asymmetric
profiles with advantage ratios near $5$. Resolving the symmetric-$n$
case on a fine grid is a computational, not conceptual, gap; we flag
it as open.

\subsection{Is it the mechanism or the paradigm?}
\label{sec:scale}

The asymmetry could conceivably be a feature of individual budgets per
se. It is not: it is a feature of \emph{how sampling-based \ac{iDP}
reconciles them}. We replay the identical game---same groups, weights,
grid, utility---through the \emph{sensitivity-based} \ac{iDP} realisation
of \citet{boenisch2023have}, in which each group receives a personal
clipping norm under a shared noise multiplier and each record's
noise-to-sensitivity ratio is preserved by construction, so $\Apld{i}$
depends on $\eps_i$ alone and the players interact only through the
shared $V(\sigma)$.

The contrast is total (\cref{fig:h2-scale}). At $w = 1$ and $K =
200$, the sensitivity-based game has \emph{exactly one} pure Nash
equilibrium, and it is symmetric: $(\eps_1, \eps_2) = (5.73, 5.73)$.
The sampling-based game on the identical grid has $48$, all
asymmetric. The same uniqueness-and-symmetry holds across the common
weight sweep ($w \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1\}$ each yield a single diagonal
\ac{NE}). This is the cleanest statement of the paper's scope: individual
privacy budgets do not by themselves create strategic redistribution;
the joint $(\sigma, p_g)$ solve of the sampling-based realisation
does. It is also, in effect, a control experiment for the theory---
remove the coupling that \cref{lem:externality} formalises, and the
Hawk--Dove structure disappears.

\centering

\caption{\textbf{Sampling-based versus sensitivity-based \ac{iDP}, same
game} ($K=200$, $w=1$). The sensitivity-based realisation (single

\subsection{Promise versus reality at a fixed contract}
\label{sec:C5}

What does the externality do to a single contract, with the strategy
stripped away? Fix player $i$'s budget at $\eps_i = 4$ and sweep only
the \emph{other} player's budget (\cref{fig:slack}). The level-1
ceiling $A_{\eps_i,\delta} = 0.963$ never moves, and the level-2
advantage stays below it everywhere---the guarantee holds---but the
gap between the levels swings from $0.79$ to $0.96$ as the other
player's budget moves, smoothly and monotonically, exactly as
\cref{prop:externality-full} predicts. The $(\eps_i, \delta)$ pair
alone reveals none of this variation---which is precisely the room
the strategic play of \cref{sec:C1,sec:C2} exploits, and the quantity
a $\Delta$-augmented contract caps (\cref{sec:mitigation}). The full
externality-response sweep across target budgets and dataset shares,
together with a finite-difference certificate that the proposition's sign
holds on $99.1\%$ of the $K=200$ grid's interior, is in
\cref{app:certificate}.

\subsection{How costly is equilibrium play?}
\label{sec:poev}

Equilibria that redistribute risk need not inflate it in aggregate; we
measure whether they do with a value-side analogue of the price of
anarchy~\cite{koutsoupias1999worst}.

\begin{definition}[Price of externality-vulnerability]
\label[definition]{def:poev}
Given a symmetric baseline profile $\boldsymbol{\eps}^{\mathrm{uni}}$
and a reference profile $\boldsymbol{\eps}^\star$,
\[
\PoEV(\boldsymbol{\eps}^\star) \;:=\;
\frac{\sum_i \Apld{i}(\boldsymbol{\eps}^\star) - \sum_i \Apld{i}(\boldsymbol{\eps}^{\mathrm{uni}})}
     {\sum_i \Apld{i}(\boldsymbol{\eps}^{\mathrm{uni}})}.
\]
\end{definition}

Against the geometric-midpoint baseline $\eps^{\mathrm{uni}} = 5.73$
(aggregate advantage $0.305$), the $48$ equilibria of the canonical
game span $\PoEV \in [-0.785, +0.040]$ with median $-0.50$
(\cref{tab:NE}): most equilibria \emph{deflate} aggregate advantage
relative to uniform play, because the utility term pulls both players
toward moderate noise. Three readings temper that comfort. First, the
aggregate hides the redistribution: the median equilibrium assigns one
player $4\times$ the advantage of the other
(\cref{sec:C1}). Second, the spread across equilibria is itself the
problem---the best equilibrium is the social optimum
($\PoEV = -0.785$; it happens to be a Nash profile, so the price of
stability is zero), the worst inflates aggregate advantage over the
baseline ($+0.04$), and nothing in the mechanism selects among them.
Third, the sign flips with the preference regime
(\cref{app:wsweep}): at utility-heavy
weights ($w \le 0.25$), $77$--$80\%$ of equilibria are inflationary,
with $\PoEV$ up to $+1.70$---rational play then \emph{raises}
aggregate exposure by up to $170\%$ over uniform budgets. Aggregate
welfare at equilibrium is, in short, unmanaged: sometimes better than
uniform, sometimes much worse, and never chosen.

\subsection{Does the game survive a measured adversary?}
\label{sec:H1}

Everything so far prices risk at the middle level of the hierarchy
\eqref{eq:hierarchy}---the mechanism-specific, \ac{PLD}-exact bound on
what any adversary can extract from the deployed mechanism. A fair question is whether
the strategic structure survives one level further down, when the
advantage is \emph{measured} instead. We instantiate the two-player
game end-to-end on Adult: for each profile of a $5 \times 5$ budget
grid ($\eps \in \{2,4,8,16,32\}$, $5000$ records per silo) we train
$128$ \ac{DPSGD} shadow models at the mechanism solution $(\sigma, p_1,
p_2)$, score per-record membership with
\ac{LiRA}~\cite{carlini2022membership}, and rebuild the payoff matrix from
the measured advantage and measured test accuracy
(\cref{fig:H1}).

The measured game degenerates---informatively. On this workload the
\ac{LiRA} advantage is flat at the measurement floor across the entire
grid ($A^{\mathrm{LiRA}} \in [0.107, 0.115]$, per-silo AUC $\le
0.506$) while the analytical bound spans $[0.004, 0.870]$: the
attacker cannot resolve the leakage the accountant prices, the
externality term of \eqref{eq:utility} is thus constant in the
players' actions, and the equilibria slide to the high-$\eps$ corner
where accuracy dominates ($(16,16)$ and $(32,32)$). The analytical
game on the same coarse grid puts its equilibrium at the
\emph{opposite}, low-budget corner $(2,2)$---a $5{\times}5$ grid is
too coarse to resolve the off-diagonal band of \cref{sec:C1}, but the
corner contrast is the point: the measured incentives are not a
perturbation of the analytical ones, they are advantage-blind. This
is the second inequality of the hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy} at its
loosest---on the $(16,16)$ profile the pooled attack ROC sits at
chance ($A_{\mathrm{LiRA}} = 0.010$) against an optimal-adversary
$A_{\mathrm{PLD}} = 0.58$, and the flat
$[0.107, 0.115]$ band of the per-record statistic is that
estimator's finite-sample floor at $128$ shadow models---and it is
the privacy-onion effect of \citet{carlini2022privacy} made
strategic: \emph{the game's asymmetric structure is carried by the
attacker's ability to realise the externality}. Two
consequences follow. For the theory's scope: the redistribution
phenomenon binds in regimes where realised attack power tracks the
accountant---strong attackers, vulnerable records, or the worst-case
adversary the contract itself must assume. For practice: a deployment
whose realistic adversary sits at the measurement floor will see no
strategic pressure on budgets---but a risk assessment cannot be
written against today's measurement floor, and the level-2 game
prices exactly the most any adversary can ever extract from the
deployed mechanism. Closing the gap between the two---a
measured instance with above-floor attack power, e.g.\ canary-style
outliers---is the natural next experiment.
