\section{Equilibrium Analysis: Equal Protection Is Unstable}
\label{sec:analysis}

As orientation before the details, the section's argument in brief:
the externality has a definite sign---tightening one player's budget
raises every other player's \ac{PLD}-advantage at their unchanged
contracts; symmetric play is unstable wherever the shared utility
is unsaturated---one knife-edge symmetric equilibrium survives
where it is not---so equilibrium protection is generically unequal;
preference heterogeneity decides in whose favour; equilibria exist in the practically
relevant regime, even though the standard potential-based existence
arguments do not apply; and coalition play recovers the known
attack. The subsections develop these claims in this order; full
proofs are deferred to \cref{app:proofs}, and the short ones stay
inline. Each claim is checked against numerical evidence in place,
so that the analysis and its verification read as one argument.

\paragraph{Numerical pipeline.}\label{sec:setup} All numerical
evidence below is analytical---no model is trained---and comes
from one pipeline. We solve the mechanism of
\cref{def:sampling-idp} by bisecting on $\sigma$ until
\eqref{eq:batch} is met, with each per-group rate chosen to make
\eqref{eq:budget} bind under the R\'enyi
accountant~\cite{mironov2017renyi}; profiles where any $p_i$ falls
below a participation threshold are flagged infeasible
(\cref{ass:participation}, \cref{app:mechanism}). Each realised
$\Apld{i}$ is then computed exactly by \ac{PLD}
accounting~\cite{google-dp-accounting}---the level-2 advantage of
\eqref{eq:hierarchy}, not the level-1 ceiling. The canonical
action grid is log-spaced with $K = 200$ points on $[0.5, 64]$ at
$\delta = 10^{-12}$; unless noted otherwise, groups are symmetric
with $|G_g| = 5000$, $B = 128$, $5$ training epochs, $\lambda_i =
1$, and $V(\sigma) = 1/(1+\sigma^2)$, mirroring the empirical
\ac{iDP} scenarios of \citet{kaiser2026your, boenisch2023have}.

\subsection{The externality is monotone}

Player $i$'s risk depends on exactly two quantities, $(p_i, \sigma)$,
and the mechanism sets both from the whole budget vector. The first
lemma fixes the \emph{direction} in which the other players' choices
move that pair.

\begin{lemma}[Privacy externality on the feasible region]
\label[lemma]{lem:externality}
Consider the sampling-based \ac{iDP} mechanism of \cref{def:sampling-idp}
on a feasible profile $\boldsymbol{\eps}$ at which constraints \eqref{eq:budget}
bind for every group and \eqref{eq:batch} binds at $B$. Then for every pair $i
\neq j$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial \eps_j} \;<\; 0
\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial \eps_j} \;<\; 0.
\label{eq:externality-lemma}
\end{equation}
When player $j$ tightens their budget, the mechanism adds noise and
samples everyone else more.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
The externality travels through what all players share: the batch
and the noise. Amplification makes the sampling rate at which group
$g$ exhausts its budget, $p_g(\sigma, \eps_g)$, increase in that
budget and in the shared $\sigma$~\cite{balle2020privacy}, so the
expected batch is strictly increasing in $\sigma$ and \eqref{eq:batch} pins
a unique $\sigma$. When player $j$ tightens their budget, they
relinquish part of the fixed batch; the solver can restore $B$ only
by raising $\sigma$, and the extra noise raises every other group's
rate. Player $i$'s mechanism has moved---in both $p_i$ and
$\sigma$---though $\eps_i$ never did. Full proof in
\cref{app:proof-externality}.
\end{proof}

The lemma does not yet determine the sign of the externality: when
player $j$ tightens their budget, player $i$ is sampled more but
under more noise, and the two effects oppose each other. The next
two results settle the sign, and both rest on the same observation.
Player $i$'s contract holds $\eps_i$ fixed and their budget constraint \eqref{eq:budget}
constraint binds at equality; this one equation ties $p_i$ to
$\sigma$, confining $i$'s operating point to the single curve in
the $(p_i, \sigma)$-plane on which the mechanism's leakage equals
$\eps_i$. To leading order this curve is the line $p_i \propto
\sigma$: at R\'enyi order $\alpha$, with $\rho_g(\eps_g,\delta)$
the group's \ac{RDP} budget~\cite{mironov2017renyi}, \eqref{eq:budget} reads
$p_g = \sigma\sqrt{\rho_g(\eps_g,\delta)}/\sqrt{2I\alpha}$, and
summing these rates across groups under \eqref{eq:batch} gives the aggregate
identity
\begin{equation}
\sigma \cdot \sum_{g}\, |G_g|\, \sqrt{\rho_g(\eps_g,\delta)} \;=\; B
\sqrt{2I\alpha}.
\label{eq:sigma-implicit}
\end{equation}
Tightening $\eps_j$ raises $\sigma$ (\cref{lem:externality}) and
moves player $i$ along this line toward larger $\sigma$. The sign
of the externality thus reduces to a single question: does
$\Apld{i}$ rise or fall along $p_i \propto \sigma$? We resolve it
first in the regime where \ac{DPSGD} operates---small sampling
rates, where the advantage admits a closed form---and then extend
it to arbitrary sampling rates.

\begin{corollary}[Externality on $\Apld{i}$, small-$p$ regime]
\label[corollary]{cor:externality-small-p}
Under the hypotheses of \cref{lem:externality}, assume additionally
$p_g \leq p_\star$ for every group, with $p_\star$ small enough that
the $O(p^2)$ remainder of the subsampling
expansion~\cite{balle2020privacy} is dominated by the leading term.
Then $\Apld{i}(p_i,\sigma) = I\, p_i\, \Adv_G(\sigma) + O\bigl((I
p_i)^2\bigr)$, where $\Adv_G(\sigma) = 2\Phi(1/(2\sigma))-1$, with
$\Phi$ the standard-normal CDF, is the advantage \eqref{eq:adv} of
the unsubsampled Gaussian mechanism at noise multiplier $\sigma$,
and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \Apld{i}}{\partial \eps_j}
   \;=\; \frac{\partial \sigma}{\partial \eps_j}
        \cdot I\, p_i \cdot
        \Bigl[\, \tfrac{\Adv_G(\sigma)}{\sigma}
              + \Adv_G'(\sigma)\, \Bigr] \;+\; O\bigl((I p_i)^2\bigr)
\;<\; 0
\label{eq:proof-bracket}
\end{equation}
for every $i \neq j$ on the small-$p$ portion of the feasible region:
tightening any other player's budget strictly raises $\Apld{i}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
The single-step Poisson-subsampled trade-off function is exactly
the mixture $T_{p,\sigma} = (1-p)(1-\alpha) + p\,T_\sigma(\alpha)$,
so its per-step sup-gap is $p\,\Adv_G(\sigma)$, which composes to
$I p_i\,\Adv_G(\sigma) + O\bigl((I p_i)^2\bigr)$. Along $p_i
\propto \sigma$ the advantage is therefore proportional to
$\sigma\,\Adv_G(\sigma)$, and the bracket in
\eqref{eq:proof-bracket} equals
$\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{d}{d\sigma}[\sigma \Adv_G(\sigma)]$, which
is strictly positive (\cref{app:proofs}). Hence $\Apld{i}$ is
strictly increasing along the line, and tightening $\eps_j$ raises
$\sigma$ and therefore $\Apld{i}$: the additional sampling
outweighs the additional noise at every $\sigma$.
\end{proof}

Beyond the small-$p$ regime this expansion no longer determines the
sign, but the line $p_i \propto \sigma$ still constrains what a
tightened $\eps_j$ can do to player $i$. Along the line the
mechanism moves, yet its leading-order (R\'enyi) privacy content
stays constant: the $(\eps_i,\delta)$ guarantee is maintained
exactly, so the contract cannot see the change. Everything happens
in the higher-order shape of the privacy profile, where the effect
consumes slack instead of violating the guarantee. A sign carried
by higher-order terms admits no closed-form proof.
\Cref{prop:externality-full} (\cref{app:proof-full-region})
therefore proves the invariance and certifies $\partial
\Apld{i}/\partial\eps_j < 0$ numerically on \emph{every} feasible
interior cell of the mechanism grid
(\cref{app:certificate})---$25{,}612$ of $25{,}612$ unit-stride
differences, in both the cross and the own direction.

In the decomposition \eqref{eq:decomp} this reads: player $i$'s
contract fixes the ceiling $A_{\eps_i,\delta}$, and the other
players decide where below the ceiling the realised advantage sits.
So far this effect had only been observed
empirically~\cite{kaiser2026your}. With a proven sign it becomes
predictable, and a predictable advantage is what allows an informed
game.

\paragraph{Promise versus reality at a fixed contract.} The
externality is visible on a single contract, with all strategy
stripped away. Fix player $i$'s budget at $\eps_i = 4$ and sweep
only the \emph{other} player's budget (\cref{fig:slack}): player
$i$'s contract, and with it their line $p_i \propto \sigma$, never
moves; every change below is the interaction moving them along it.
The contracted vulnerability $A_{\eps_i,\delta} = 0.963$ stands
still, and $\Apld{i}$ stays below it everywhere---the guarantee
holds---but the slack between the two swings from $0.79$ to
$0.96$, smoothly and monotonically, exactly as
\cref{prop:externality-full} predicts. The $(\eps_i, \delta)$ pair
alone reveals none of this variation; it is the room the strategic
play of \cref{sec:no-symmetric-ne,sec:self-sorting} exploits, and
the quantity a $\Delta$-augmented contract caps
(\cref{sec:mitigation}). The full externality-response sweep, and
the exhaustive sign certificate, are in \cref{app:certificate}.

\subsection{No symmetric equilibrium}
\label{sec:no-symmetric-ne}

The externality's sign says how one player's choice moves the
other's slack \eqref{eq:decomp}; it does not say where play comes
to rest. Equal protection is feasible: on every diagonal profile
of the symmetric game the two slacks coincide. Do the players
settle there? At exactly one budget, yes; everywhere else, no.

\begin{proposition}[Diagonal trough outside utility saturation]
\label[proposition]{prop:no-symmetric-ne}
Consider the two-player \ac{PEG} with $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 =
\lambda > 0$ and $|G_1| = |G_2|$, in the small-$p$ regime of
\cref{cor:externality-small-p}. Assume $V \in C^2$ with $V' < 0$,
and that the shared utility is not yet saturated at the diagonal
operating point:
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2\, V''(\sigma) \;+\; 2\,\sigma\, V'(\sigma) \;\geq\; 0,
\label{eq:sign-condition}
\end{equation}
which for the canonical $V(\sigma) = 1/(1+\sigma^2)$ holds exactly
on $\sigma \geq \sqrt{3}$. Then no interior diagonal profile
$(\eps^\star, \eps^\star)$ with strict feasibility is a pure Nash
equilibrium.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
In the root-budget variable $s_i := \sqrt{\rho(\eps_i,\delta)}$,
identity \eqref{eq:sigma-implicit} makes the mechanism an
aggregator: $\sigma = C/(s_1+s_2)$ and $p_i = C'\,s_i/(s_1+s_2)$
for positive constants $C, C'$---each player's sampling rate is
their share of the aggregate. On the diagonal the two first-order
conditions coincide, and two exact identities decide the
second-order test (\cref{app:proof-no-symmetric}): the difference
$\partial^2_{s_1} u_1 - \partial_{s_1}\partial_{s_2} u_1$ isolates
the share term, the sum assembles the full diagonal curvature
\begin{equation}
4 s^2\, \frac{\partial^2 u_1}{\partial s_1^2}
\;=\; \bigl[\sigma^2 V'' + 2\sigma V'\bigr]
\;+\; \lambda \cdot (\textrm{own-risk curvature}),
\label{eq:diag-curvature-main}
\end{equation}
and in the small-$p$ regime the own-risk curvature is strictly
positive (a two-line Gaussian computation). Under
\eqref{eq:sign-condition} both terms are non-negative and the first-order
candidate is a strict local \emph{minimum} of each player's own
utility: deviating in either direction pays. Diagonal points that
are not critical fail the first-order test outright. Full proof in
\cref{app:proof-no-symmetric}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Where saturation wins: one symmetric equilibrium.} The
saturation condition is not decoration. $V$ is bounded, so joint
loosening buys less and less; below $\sigma = \sqrt 3$ the shared-utility
curvature in \eqref{eq:diag-curvature-main} turns negative, and
where it outweighs the risk curvature the trough flips to a peak.
The canonical instance realises the flip: the operating noise
satisfies $\sigma < \sqrt 3$ at every diagonal first-order
candidate, the measured curvature is negative on a window around
$\eps \approx 3$, and inside it the game has \emph{exactly one}
symmetric pure equilibrium, at $\eps^\star = 2.96$---confirmed on a
$25$-point local refinement of the action grid, every deviation
losing (\cref{app:proof-no-symmetric}). Everywhere else the
diagonal remains a trough: the median best-deviation gain across
the $200$ diagonal cells is $0.122$ utility, and alternating
best-response dynamics started on the diagonal leave it at the
first response in $197$ of $200$ runs---the other three start
inside the window and stay---settling after a median of two steps
at an off-diagonal pure equilibrium (\cref{app:symbreak}).

To see the proposition at work, play the game from any starting
profile. A player asks one question: does the last unit of budget
buy more model utility than it costs in weighted own advantage?
The balance point is the interior best response,
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\lambda_i}
\;=\; \frac{ \partial \Apld{i} / \partial \eps_i }
          { V'(\sigma)\;\partial \sigma / \partial \eps_i }
\;\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\eps}},
\label{eq:foc}
\end{equation}
and the direction of the imbalance is the direction of the move:
while the marginal utility exceeds the weighted marginal
advantage, loosen; once it falls short, tighten. From here the
walk to an equilibrium has four steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{On the diagonal, both directions pay---outside the
saturation window.} \Cref{prop:no-symmetric-ne} puts every
unsaturated diagonal candidate at the bottom of a trough of each
player's own utility, so the question above has the worst possible
answer---both. On the $K = 200$ cache the trough is the rule: the
median best-deviation gain across the $200$ diagonal cells is
$0.122$ utility. Someone moves---unless play starts inside the
window around $\eps^\star = 2.96$, where nothing pays and the
symmetric equilibrium holds.

\item \textbf{One loosening, two ledgers.} Suppose player~1
loosens; everything that follows is the aggregator
\eqref{eq:sigma-implicit}. For player~1 the move is a new
contract: a steeper line $p_1 \propto \sigma$ and a larger share
of the fixed batch. The noise falls, so $V$ rises for both
players, and player~1 is sampled more under that thinner noise,
so $\Apld{1}$ rises---player~1 buys the shared utility with their
own exposure. Player~2 signs nothing and does nothing, yet gains
twice: their line is unchanged, the falling $\sigma$ slides them
down it, and along the line less sampling beats less noise
(\cref{cor:externality-small-p})---$\Apld{2}$ falls while $V$
rises.

\item \textbf{The counter-move is to tighten.} The model
improvement player~2 would have loosened for has already been
delivered, so the utility side of \eqref{eq:foc} has collapsed
while the advantage side has not: budgets are strategic
substitutes, and player~2's best response is to tighten---shed
exposure, and consume what player~1 funded.

\item \textbf{Play settles at a crossing, generically off the
diagonal.} By step~3, near the diagonal each best response falls
in the other's budget, and an equilibrium is a crossing of the two
mirror-image loci (\cref{fig:C6}). Outside the saturation window
the diagonal threads the gap between them: no symmetric profile is
a mutual best response, so alternating best responses leave the
diagonal at the first move---in $197$ of $200$ runs started
there---and settle, in a median of two steps, where the loci
cross: one player loose and exposed, one tight and protected. The
three exceptions start inside the window and stay at the symmetric
equilibrium (\cref{app:symbreak}).
\end{enumerate}

Unlike the externality results, this subsection needs more of $V$
than its sign: the saturation curvature \eqref{eq:sign-condition}
is load-bearing, and it is exactly where it fails that symmetric
play survives.

\paragraph{Enumeration agrees: one symmetric equilibrium, all
others asymmetric.} We enumerate the cleanest instance by brute
force: two players, identical group sizes, identical weights, both
budgets swept over the full grid. The $K = 200$ payoff matrix has
$43$ pure Nash equilibria: the symmetric equilibrium of the
saturation window, and $42$ off-diagonal equilibria in mirror
pairs (\cref{fig:C6}; representative equilibria in \cref{tab:NE}).
The asymmetric equilibria are \emph{frontier} equilibria: at all
$42$, the tight player sits within one grid cell of the
participation floor against the loose player's budget---the same
feasible infimum the coalition best response of
\cref{sec:coalition} selects---so the budget ratios concentrate in
the narrow band the frontier's geometry dictates,
$\max(\eps_1^*,\eps_2^*)/\min(\eps_1^*,\eps_2^*) \in [6.38, 6.70]$
with median $6.54$. Stable asymmetric play means one player
roughly $6.5\times$ looser, and the \ac{PLD}-advantages split
accordingly---at the most asymmetric equilibria one player's
realised $A$ reaches $0.32$ while the other's stays below $0.001$.
\Cref{fig:C1} shows the incentive at work: at any diagonal
profile outside the window, lowering the own budget buys a large
drop in own advantage at a modest utility cost.

The \ac{BR} loci in \cref{fig:C6} are the zero set of
\eqref{eq:foc}, read off the cached payoff by numeric
differentiation along each player's own axis: neither side of
\eqref{eq:foc} has a closed form, but the sign of their difference
is measurable in every cell. The corner branches are not interior
optima: against a sufficiently loose opponent, the best response is
pinned to the participation frontier
(\cref{ass:participation})---the same feasible infimum the
coalition best response of \cref{sec:coalition} selects---and
against a sufficiently tight one, to the frontier's mirror. The
frontier itself is analytic. On it, player~2's rate sits at the
floor $\bar p$, so the batch constraint pins player~1's rate at
$B/|G_1| - \bar p$; the noise $\bar\sigma(\eps_1)$ then solves
player~1's budget alone, and the frontier is the curve on which
$(\bar\sigma(\eps_1), \bar p)$ exhausts exactly $\eps_2$. So on
two of its three branches the best response is a closed-form
curve, and the equilibria inherit that structure: the tight
player's coordinate is exact, the loose player's is a
near-indifferent position along the wall---the loose payoff varies
by less than $10^{-4}$ across the highlighted segment, so the $42$
grid equilibria are best read as samples from a one-parameter
family of frontier equilibria. Grid
resolution moves the equilibrium
\emph{count}, not the structure: at $K = 22$ the same game
resolves $7$ pure \acp{NE}, at $K = 200$ it resolves $43$---the
stepped \ac{BR} loci of a finer grid cross more often. What is
invariant, and is the claim we stake, is the structure: mirror
pairs along the crossing band, plus the single symmetric
equilibrium of the saturation window, already present at $K = 22$;
we therefore quote $K = 200$ throughout and read individual
\ac{NE} coordinates as samples from the crossing band. The common
privacy weight moves the picture only at its extremes: $\lambda
\in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1\}$ yields $34$--$62$ pure \acp{NE} of
the same structure, but at $\lambda = 2$ the grid has \emph{no}
pure equilibrium at all---only mixed play survives---and at
$\lambda = 4$ a single corner equilibrium $(0.5, 0.5)$ remains
(\cref{app:wsweep}).

\paragraph{What survives of symmetry.} Two things, of very
different character. Inside the saturation window, symmetry
survives outright: the pure equilibrium at $\eps^\star = 2.96$
gives both players the same budget and the same realised advantage
($0.104$ each), and it is the equilibrium with the least aggregate
advantage of the entire set (\cref{sec:poev}). Outside the window,
symmetry survives only as a lottery. Restricted to the two budgets
of a mirror pair---take $(1.12, 7.13)$---the \ac{PEG} is a
Hawk--Dove game~\cite{maynardsmith1973logic}: each player prefers
the tight, protected role ($u = 0.50$, against $0.25$ for the
exposed role), both symmetric profiles of the restriction are
unstable, and the restriction's only symmetric equilibrium is
mixed~\cite{nash1951noncooperative}, playing tight with
probability $0.45$ at value $0.35$---strictly between the two role
payoffs (\cref{app:symbreak}). That lottery is fair, but every
draw of it hands one player the exposed role. Any asymmetry
between the players breaks the tie and selects the convention;
preference asymmetry is the one our players have, and we turn to
it next.

\subsection{Whose slack shrinks: self-sorting}
\label{sec:self-sorting}

Under exact symmetry the equilibria come in mirror pairs, and which
player concedes is settled only by the lottery over roles of
\cref{sec:no-symmetric-ne}. Any preference asymmetry breaks the
tie, and it breaks it in a fixed direction.

\begin{proposition}[Self-sorting, 2-player \ac{PEG}]
\label[proposition]{prop:self-sorting}
Consider the 2-player \ac{PEG} with $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > 0$
(player~1 the more privacy-sensitive), $|G_1| = |G_2|$, $V$
strictly decreasing, own-budget monotonicity $\partial \Apld{i} / \partial
\eps_i > 0$, and the exchange condition (S) of
\cref{app:proof-self-sorting}. Then every pure Nash equilibrium
$(\eps_1^\star, \eps_2^\star)$ satisfies $\eps_1^\star \le
\eps_2^\star$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Suppose the \emph{more} privacy-sensitive player held the higher
budget. At equilibrium neither player can profit by swapping to the
other's budget; dividing the two resulting no-swap inequalities and
applying condition (S) forces $\lambda_2 \geq \lambda_1$---a
contradiction. Full proof in \cref{app:proof-self-sorting}.
\end{proof}

Condition (S) holds throughout the high-noise regime and is
verified numerically at every equilibrium candidate we report
(\cref{app:proof-self-sorting}).

\paragraph{The sweep confirms the sorting.} Fixing $\lambda_2 = 1$
and sweeping $\lambda_1 \in \{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$ yields
$37$--$61$ pure \acp{NE} per ratio (\cref{fig:C8}). The \ac{NE}
cloud migrates exactly as \cref{prop:self-sorting} predicts: when
player~1 values privacy less, the equilibria sit in the region
where player~1 holds the high budget and the higher realised
advantage; as $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$ crosses unity, the cloud
migrates through the symmetric configuration into the mirror
region. Sorting compliance---the fraction of equilibria with the
higher-$\lambda$ player on the lower budget---is $170$ of $174$
across the sweep, with all four exceptions at the mildest ratio
$\lambda_1/\lambda_2 = 0.5$.
Preference is not the only asymmetry that assigns the roles.
Sweeping the group-size ratio $r = |G_1|/|G_2|$ across two orders
of magnitude (all else symmetric), the smaller group plays the
smaller equilibrium budget and ends at the smaller realised
advantage at every ratio (\cref{fig:C2}): with player~1 in the
minority ($r < 1$), equilibria have $\eps_1^* \in [1.5, 3]$
against $\eps_2^* \approx 10$, yielding $\Apld{1}^* < 0.01$
against $\Apld{2}^* \in [0.24, 0.32]$, and the pattern mirrors for
$r > 1$. The mechanism is legible in \eqref{eq:batch}: a small
group demanding strong privacy contributes little to the expected
batch, the majority's sampling rate rises to compensate, and its
realised advantage rises with it. The minority free-rides on the
shared mechanism~\cite{hardin1968tragedy}---a privacy analogue of
the classical result that small groups benefit disproportionately
from a common resource.

exposed) to upper-left (player $2$ exposed), never crossing the
diagonal: heterogeneity self-sorts instead of averaging out.}
\label{fig:C8}
\end{figure}

The direction is fixed: the \emph{more} privacy-sensitive player
ends at the lower budget \emph{and} the lower realised advantage;
the slack that shrinks belongs to the player who cares least. The
exposed player is not exploited---at equilibrium the shared model
utility compensates them. But the outcome is one the \ac{iDP}
contract never priced: your realised protection is set by your
neighbours' preferences, not your own.

\subsection{Existence, and why enumeration is necessary}
\label{sec:existence}

Symmetry breaking says where equilibria are not; it does not yet
show that any exist. In the low-sampling region where \ac{DPSGD}
operates, the same substitutes structure that pushes the players
apart also delivers existence.

\begin{theorem}[Pure \ac{NE} existence on a substitutes box, $n = 2$]
\label{thm:continuous-ne-smallp}
Let $\mathcal{B} = [\eps_{\mathrm{lo}}, \eps_{\mathrm{hi}}]^2$ be a box of
feasible profiles on which $p_g \leq p_\star$ (as in
\cref{cor:externality-small-p}) and the budgets are strategic
substitutes, $\partial^2 u_i / \partial \eps_1 \partial \eps_2 \leq
0$. Then the \ac{PEG} restricted to $\mathcal{B}$ has a pure Nash
equilibrium~\cite{topkis1979equilibrium,
milgrom1990rationalizability}, and any such equilibrium whose best
responses over the full action set remain in $\mathcal{B}$ is an
equilibrium of the \ac{PEG} itself.
\end{theorem}

Both hypotheses hold in the practically relevant regime:
\ac{DPSGD} sampling rates sit at $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$, placing
operation in the small-$p$ regime of
\cref{cor:externality-small-p}, and there the substitutes condition
reduces, for the canonical $V$, to the curvature bound $\sigma \leq
\sqrt 3$, which holds on the less-noisy portion of the caches'
operating range $\sigma \in [0.5, 5]$ (proof and the lattice
structure of the equilibrium set in \cref{app:proofs}).

Two limitations remain. Existence on the full feasible region,
including the saturation corner $p_g \to 1$, is open; there we rely
on exhaustive grid enumeration (\cref{sec:no-symmetric-ne}). And this
order-theoretic (lattice) route is intrinsically two-player:
substitutes cannot be
re-ordered into complements for every pair at once when $n > 2$. The
aggregative structure of \eqref{eq:sigma-implicit}---opponents enter
only through a sum---is the natural route to larger games, in the
Cournot tradition of \citet{novshek1985cournot}; we leave that
route open and use enumeration and fictitious play at $n > 2$
(\cref{sec:H3}).

Enumeration is a necessity, not a convenience: the familiar
shortcut---a potential function whose minimiser is automatically an
equilibrium---is unavailable in principle.

\begin{proposition}[The \ac{PEG} is not a potential game]
\label[proposition]{prop:non-potential}
Under \eqref{eq:utility} and the mechanism of \cref{def:sampling-idp},
the \ac{PEG} admits no exact, weighted, or ordinal potential in the sense
of \citet{monderer1996potential}.
\end{proposition}

The Price-of-Privacy game was a potential game precisely because its
privacy cost was separable~\cite{pejo2019together}; the mechanism's
coupling is what destroys the structure (proof in \cref{app:proofs}).
This is why our existence route trades the concavity that the
classical existence arguments require for the monotonicity that
strategic substitutes provide, and why the sign analysis of
\cref{cor:externality-small-p,prop:externality-full} is the
load-bearing input.

\paragraph{Where $V$ enters as more than a sign.} The externality
proofs never touch $V$, the best-response condition \eqref{eq:foc}
uses only $\mathrm{sgn}\,V'$, and the coalition corollary of
\cref{sec:coalition} uses only the externality sign on the
target's coordinate; \cref{prop:V-invariance} (\cref{app:V})
records this formally. The one exception is deliberate: the
symmetry-breaking result of \cref{sec:no-symmetric-ne} reads $V$'s
saturation curvature \eqref{eq:sign-condition}, and the location
of the symmetric equilibrium moves with the shape of $V$
(\cref{app:v-shape}). The experimental choice $V(\sigma) =
1/(1+\sigma^2)$ is a representative of a class for every
sign-driven result, and the explicit curvature condition states
exactly what the exception needs.

\subsection{Coalitions: the attack is a corollary}
\label{sec:coalition}

Every player so far weighs only their own harm. We now let a subset
commit to a joint action first, with the remaining players
responding---a Stackelberg game with malice weight $\kappa$ on the
target's vulnerability (\cref{def:coalition})---and read off the
coalition's best response in the high-malice limit.

\begin{corollary}[Coalition best response recovers the attack]
\label[corollary]{cor:coalition-attack}
Fix a target $t$, a coalition $C \subseteq N \setminus \{t\}$ of size
$k$, and any actions $\boldsymbol{\eps}_{-C}$ for the others. As
$\kappa \to \infty$ in \cref{def:coalition}, the coalition's
Stackelberg best response is coalition-symmetric---every colluder
plays the same budget---at the \emph{participation-preserving
minimum} $\eps^{\star}_k(\boldsymbol{\eps}_{-C})$, and the target
advantage
$\Apld{t}$ induced at that best response is non-decreasing in $k$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
$\partial \Apld{t} / \partial \eps_i < 0$ for every $i \in C$
(\cref{prop:externality-full}; analytic at small $p$, certified
numerically beyond), so the coalition pushes every budget as low as
feasibility admits. Below the participation floor a colluder's
sampling rate reaches zero (\cref{ass:participation}), so they drop
out of \eqref{eq:batch} and stop raising $\sigma$---pushing lower only
forfeits leverage; the optimum is therefore the \emph{feasible
infimum}, not the grid minimum. Adding a member enlarges the set of
budgets pulling $\sigma$ up, so $\Apld{t}$ is non-decreasing in $k$.
\end{proof}

This is the budget-manipulation attack of \citet{kaiser2026your},
recovered as rational play: any coordinated subset of data holders
whose joint utility weights the target's harm arrives at the same
action vector, whether or not anyone calls it an attack. The
equivalence is all the corollary claims---the empirical
demonstration is prior work's---and \cref{sec:attacks} shows the
attack's force is contingent on a target that does not respond.
